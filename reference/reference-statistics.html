Statistics Reference

Every term in the strand, in plain English

A lookup sheet, not a tutorial. Each term has a plain-English meaning, a Tidebreak example and the mistake people make with it. Every number on this page is computed from the playtest data when the site is built. The notes teach these ideas; this page is for checking one quickly during a lab.

Describing data

Term What it means Tidebreak example The common mistake
Observation One unit you measured. Read it as: one row. One player in one session: 120 observations in tidebreak_sessions.csv. Mixing units in one table, such as session rows and per-player totals side by side.
Variable One thing recorded about every observation. Read it as: one column. session_minutes, cohort, deaths: 26 variables in all. Spreading one variable across several columns, as a survey export does with its questions.
Categorical Values are labels. Nominal labels have no order; ordinal labels do, with unknown gaps. quit_reason is nominal, with 4 categories. cohort is ordinal: Novice, Regular, Veteran. Averaging a category because it was coded as 1, 2, 3.
Numerical Values are quantities, so arithmetic on them means something. score, deaths, session_minutes. Treating an ID or a 1–5 rating as a plain number without thinking about it.
Discrete Numerical values that are counts: whole numbers only. deaths runs from 0 to 7. Drawing a histogram with more bars than there are possible values, which leaves gaps that look like a pattern.
Continuous Numerical values that can fall anywhere in a range. session_minutes, frames$ms. Tabulating it with table(); almost every value is unique. Use a histogram.
Sample The observations you actually have. 120 sessions from 30 players. Forgetting to report how many (rule 8).
Population Everyone the conclusion is meant to apply to. You never measure it. Everyone who will play Tidebreak after launch. Assuming the sample looks like the population. A studio’s Discord is not its audience.

Centre and spread

Term What it means Tidebreak example The common mistake
Mean The total divided by the count. Read it as: the value if everyone were equal. Mean session length 22.8 minutes. Quoting it for skewed data, where a few extreme values drag it away from most players.
Median The middle value when sorted. Half above, half below. Median session length 26.1 minutes. Assuming it is always better. It ignores how extreme the extremes are, which is sometimes the point.
Minimum / maximum The smallest and largest values. Session length from 7.7 to 32 minutes. Skipping them. They are the fastest way to spot an impossible value.
Range Maximum minus minimum. 24.3 minutes for session length. Using it as the measure of spread. One unusual player sets it.
Standard deviation The typical distance of a value from the mean. Frame time: 1.05 ms in Build A, 3.40 ms in Build B. Quoting it for skewed data. It inherits every weakness of the mean.
Interquartile range (IQR) The width of the middle half of the data. 13.8 minutes for session length. Forgetting it exists. For skewed data, quote the median and IQR together.
Percentile The value with that percentage of the data below it. 99th percentile frame time: 19.5 ms in Build A, 39.4 ms in Build B. Stopping at the 95th, which cannot see a problem rarer than one case in twenty.

Shape

Term What it means Tidebreak example The common mistake
Distribution The full pattern of values: which occur and how often. A histogram draws it. Sentinel score has two humps: games that anchor the round and games that collapse early. Summarising a two-humped distribution with one average, which describes neither group (rule 4).
Skew A distribution with a long tail on one side. The mean is pulled towards the tail. Session length is left-skewed: mean 22.8 below median 26.1, pulled down by early quits. Reading the gap between mean and median without comparing it to the size of the variable.
Outlier A value far from the rest. A boxplot draws each one as a point. The boxplot of score flags 7 low values, all of them Sentinel games. Deleting it. An outlier is an error, an extreme player or the finding; find out which first (rule 6).

From sample to population

Term What it means Tidebreak example The common mistake
Sampling variability A different sample gives different numbers, even from the same population. Split the sessions into four batches of 30 in file order: mean scores 1550, 1313, 1462, 1390. Treating one playtest’s numbers as fixed facts about the game.
Confidence interval A range of plausible values for the population, given the sample. Wider means less certain. Build B sessions ran between 0.2 and 5.7 minutes longer than Build A (95% interval). Reading “95%” as the chance this particular interval is right. It describes how often the method captures the true value.

Testing a difference

Term What it means Tidebreak example The common mistake
Null hypothesis The claim that there is no difference, which the test tries to find evidence against. Build A and Build B have the same mean session length. Believing the test can prove it. Failing to find a difference is not showing there is none.
Alternative hypothesis The claim that there is a difference. Mean session length differs between the builds. Choosing a one-sided direction after seeing which way the data went.
p-value How surprising the data would be if the null hypothesis were true. Small means surprising. Session length by build: p = 0.037. Reading it as the probability that the null hypothesis is true. It is not.
Statistical significance A p-value below a threshold set in advance, conventionally 0.05. Session length differs (p = 0.037); score does not (p = 0.822). Treating “significant” as “important”. It means “unlikely to be chance alone”, nothing more.
Practical significance Whether a difference is big enough to change a design decision. In the Atrium, Build B’s frames are 0.42 ms faster (p < 0.001): highly significant and imperceptible to any player. Reporting a tiny, significant difference as a win. Always give the size of the difference too.
Independent-samples t-test Compares two group means when different people are in each group. t.test(y ~ group) t.test(session_minutes ~ build): every player saw only one build. Ignoring that each player contributed 4 sessions, so the sessions are not fully independent of one another.
Paired t-test Compares two measurements taken on the same people. t.test(x, y, paired = TRUE) Not possible on this playtest: 0 of 30 players played both builds. Running a paired test on unpaired data, or an independent test when the same players did both.

Relationships

Term What it means Tidebreak example The common mistake
Correlation How closely two numerical variables move together, from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (together). Awareness and score: 0.59, the strongest of the ability ratings. Shots fired and score: 0.05. Mobility and survivability: -0.66, a trade-off. Calling a correlation “strong” without context. Whether 0.3 matters depends on the question.
Confounding variable A third variable that drives both of the variables you are comparing. Veterans average 75.6 awareness against 57.3 for Novices, and a median score of 1664 against 1281. Experience could be driving both. Reporting a relationship without asking what else differs between the players at each end of it.
Correlation versus causation Two things moving together does not show that one causes the other. Buffing awareness would not necessarily raise score; the players with high awareness may simply be better players. Turning “is associated with” into “causes” between the analysis and the design meeting.

Which tool for which question

The question Look at Summarise with Test with
What was a typical session like? Histogram Median and IQR —
How bad does the worst case get? Histogram or trace 95th and 99th percentiles —
Do two builds differ, with different players on each? Side-by-side boxplots Medians, IQRs, difference in means Independent-samples t-test
Do two builds differ, with the same players on both? Each player’s difference Mean difference Paired t-test
Do two numerical variables move together? Scatterplot Correlation —
Is a difference worth acting on? The size of the difference and its confidence interval — No test answers this; it is a design judgement
