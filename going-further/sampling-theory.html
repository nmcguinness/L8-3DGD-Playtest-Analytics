Sampling Theory

The standard error formula, the central limit theorem, and the bootstrap

Going further. This page extends From Sample to Population. Nothing here is needed for the labs or the assessment. Read it if you want to know why the standard error and the confidence interval work, or if you need an interval for something other than a mean.

The Standard Error Formula

The standard error can be estimated from a single sample:

\[\text{SE} = \frac{\text{SD}}{\sqrt{n}}\]

Check it against the simulation on the notes page. The population SD is 7.73, so for n = 30 the formula gives 7.73 / √30 = 1.41. The SD of a thousand simulated playtest means is 1.37. The formula works.

Bigger samples give smaller standard errors, but slowly. Because n is under a square root, you need four times the players to halve the standard error:

par(mar = c(4, 6, 1, 1))
stripchart(list("n = 10" = means_10, "n = 30" = means_30, "n = 100" = means_100),
           method = "jitter", jitter = 0.25, pch = 16, cex = 0.4, las = 1,
           col = adjustcolor(pal$line, alpha.f = 0.3),
           xlab = "Playtest mean session length (minutes)")
abline(v = pop_mean, lty = 2, lwd = 2, col = pal$highlight)
Players per playtest SE from the formula SD of 1,000 simulated means
10 2.44 2.45
30 1.41 1.37
100 0.77 0.79

The Central Limit Theorem

Session length is lopsided and two-humped, nothing like a bell. Yet the means of playtests drawn from it form a bell almost perfectly:

par(mar = c(4, 3, 1, 1))
hist(means_30, breaks = 30, freq = FALSE, col = pal$fill, border = "white",
     main = "", xlab = "Mean session length (minutes)", ylab = "", yaxt = "n")
curve(dnorm(x, mean(means_30), sd(means_30)), add = TRUE, lwd = 2,
      col = pal$highlight)

That is the central limit theorem: whatever shape the individual values have, the means of reasonably large samples are approximately normal.

It is why the normal distribution matters even for data that is not normal. The 68–95–99.7 rule does not describe the session lengths, but it does describe the sampling distribution of their mean. So about 95% of playtest means fall within 2 standard errors of the true mean. That single fact is what confidence intervals and t-tests are built on.

How large is “reasonably large”? For mildly skewed data, 30 is usually plenty. For heavily skewed data, such as frame times with rare hitches, it takes more. And it applies to means: it does not rescue a 99th percentile.

The Interval by Hand

The “about 2” in “mean ± about 2 SE” is 1.96 for large samples. For smaller ones R uses a slightly larger number from the t distribution, which allows for the SD itself being estimated from the sample. The part after the ± is the margin of error.

m  <- mean(mins_b)
se <- sd(mins_b) / sqrt(length(mins_b))
multiplier <- qt(0.975, df = length(mins_b) - 1)

c(mean = m, se = se, multiplier = multiplier)
      mean         se multiplier 
24.2416667  0.8962864  2.0009954 
m + c(-1, 1) * multiplier * se
[1] 22.44820 26.03513

The same as t.test(mins_b)$conf.int: 22.4 to 26.

\[\text{95\% CI} = \bar{x} \pm t_{0.975,\,n-1} \times \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\]

A 99% interval uses a larger multiplier, so it is wider: more confidence costs precision.

The Bootstrap

The formula relies on the central limit theorem. The bootstrap gets an interval without it, by treating your sample as a stand-in for the population and resampling it. Draw 60 sessions with replacement from Build B’s 60, take the mean, and repeat a thousand times; the middle 95% of those means is the interval.

set.seed(3)
boot_means <- replicate(1000, mean(sample(mins_b, replace = TRUE)))
quantile(boot_means, c(0.025, 0.975))
    2.5%    97.5% 
22.59825 25.83671

It agrees closely with t.test()’s 22.4 to 26. The bootstrap earns its keep for summaries with no simple formula, such as a median or a 99th percentile: replace mean with median in the line above and it still works.

set.seed(3)
boot_medians <- replicate(1000, median(sample(mins_b, replace = TRUE)))
quantile(boot_medians, c(0.025, 0.975))
   2.5%   97.5% 
24.7975 28.1500
