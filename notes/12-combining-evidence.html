Combining Evidence

Telemetry, observation and self-report, coded, compared and reported honestly

Workflow
  1. Question
  2. Measure
  3. Collect
  4. Clean
  5. Describe
  6. Compare/Relate
  7. Interpret
  8. Act

A single measure can mislead you. This page is about putting the three sources of playtest evidence side by side: what the game recorded, what you saw, and what the players said. It covers turning free-text comments into codes you can count, checking a finding against more than one source, and writing honest limitations. The playtest question running through it: “The telemetry says Build B fixed navigation. Do the players agree?”

Stage: 4 · Core. Read before the March workshop. Time: 25 minutes, then the workshop lab on your own data. Before this: Planning a Playtest for the three sources, and Relationships and Correlation for the navigation-rating finding. Key terms: telemetry, observation, self-report, triangulation, code, codebook, limitation. R you will write: case_when() with grepl() inside mutate(), then count().

Learning Outcomes

By the end of these notes you can:

  • say what each of telemetry, observation and self-report can and cannot tell you;
  • turn free-text comments into a small set of codes with a written codebook, and count them;
  • use quotes as evidence without letting one quote become the finding;
  • triangulate a finding across sources, and say what it means when they disagree;
  • write limitations that are specific to your playtest.

Why This Matters for Playtesting

By the end of a project your team has numbers from the game, notes from watching people play, survey ratings and a pile of comments. It is tempting to report the source that tells the best story. The reader cannot see what you left out.

The strongest findings use more than one source. “Players got lost less” from telemetry alone is a count. Add an observer who saw players stop backtracking at the junction, and players who wrote that the route was obvious, and it is a finding nobody will argue with.

The most interesting findings are where the sources disagree. Tidebreak has one. It is the worked example at the end of this page.

Three Sources, Three Questions

Source Answers Strength Weakness
Telemetry What happened in the game? Complete, precise, cheap at scale Says nothing about why, or how it felt
Observation How did the player’s behaviour unfold? Sees hesitation, confusion and workarounds that leave no trace in a log Slow; one observer sees one player; observers see what they expect
Self-report What does the player say they experienced? The only source for feelings, intentions and opinions Players misremember, are polite, and do not notice everything

Choose measures from at least two sources for every question that matters. That decision is made when you plan the playtest, not when you write the report (Planning a Playtest).

Recording observation

Observation is only evidence if it is recorded the same way for every player. Write a short observation sheet before the playtest, with the behaviours you expect to matter, and tick or time-stamp them as they happen:

Time Location Behaviour Note
02:10 First junction Backtracked Went left, returned
02:45 First junction Hesitated > 5 s Looked at the ceiling lights
06:30 Corridor Asked a question “Where am I meant to go?”

Fix the behaviour list in advance: backtracked, hesitated more than 5 seconds, asked for help, ignored a prompt. Then observations can be counted per player, exactly like telemetry, and joined to it on the player ID.

For your project: if your team watched playtests but did not write anything down, say so in the limitations. Next time, take an observation sheet with three to five behaviours on it.

Coding Comments

Free text is the richest self-report you have, and the hardest to use. Coding turns it into something you can count: read the comments, decide on a small set of codes, and give each comment one.

Step 1: read everything first

Before writing a single code, read every comment. Tidebreak has 120 comments, but only 10 different sentences:

sessions |> count(comment, sort = TRUE)
# A tibble: 10 × 2
   comment                                     n
   <chr>                                   <int>
 1 Died a lot but it felt fair.               22
 2 Ran in circles for a while.                16
 3 Got lost after the first room.             15
 4 No idea what the objective was.            13
 5 Too hard for a first attempt.              13
 6 Could not tell where I was meant to go.    10
 7 Enjoyed it, would play again.              10
 8 Lighting made the exit easy to spot.       10
 9 The route was obvious this time.            6
10 The signposting helped.                     5

Real comments will be messier, longer and more varied. Read them anyway.

Step 2: write a codebook

A codebook lists each code, what counts as that code, and an example. Keep it to four to six codes; more than that and nobody can apply them consistently.

Code Use when the comment… Example
Lost or unclear says the player did not know where to go or what to do “Ran in circles for a while.”
Found the way says navigation or guidance worked “Lighting made the exit easy to spot.”
Difficulty is about dying, challenge or fairness “Too hard for a first attempt.”
General is anything else “Enjoyed it, would play again.”

The codebook goes in an appendix. A reader needs to see your codes to judge your counts, exactly as they need your cleaning log to judge your n.

Step 3: apply the codes in R

grepl() returns TRUE when a comment contains any of the words you give it, and case_when() gives each comment the first code that matches:

coded <- sessions |>
  mutate(code = case_when(
    grepl("lost|circles|meant to go|objective", comment) ~ "Lost or unclear",
    grepl("route|exit|signposting", comment)             ~ "Found the way",
    grepl("Died|hard", comment)                          ~ "Difficulty",
    TRUE                                                 ~ "General"
  ))

coded |> count(code)
# A tibble: 4 × 2
  code                n
  <chr>           <int>
1 Difficulty         35
2 Found the way      21
3 General            10
4 Lost or unclear    54

| inside the quotes means “or”. TRUE ~ "General" catches everything left over.

Step 4: check by hand

Keyword matching is fast and blunt. Read a sample of comments next to their codes, and fix the codebook or the keywords until they agree with what you would have decided:

coded |> distinct(comment, code)
# A tibble: 10 × 2
   comment                                 code           
   <chr>                                   <chr>          
 1 Could not tell where I was meant to go. Lost or unclear
 2 The signposting helped.                 Found the way  
 3 Lighting made the exit easy to spot.    Found the way  
 4 Got lost after the first room.          Lost or unclear
 5 Too hard for a first attempt.           Difficulty     
 6 Died a lot but it felt fair.            Difficulty     
 7 Ran in circles for a while.             Lost or unclear
 8 The route was obvious this time.        Found the way  
 9 No idea what the objective was.         Lost or unclear
10 Enjoyed it, would play again.           General

With real comments, check at least twenty. If two people on the team code the same twenty comments separately and disagree often, the codebook is not clear enough yet.

Step 5: count by group, then read what you counted

coded |> count(code, build)
# A tibble: 8 × 3
  code            build     n
  <chr>           <chr> <int>
1 Difficulty      A        19
2 Difficulty      B        16
3 Found the way   A        12
4 Found the way   B         9
5 General         A         6
6 General         B         4
7 Lost or unclear A        23
8 Lost or unclear B        31

Codes are counts of what players chose to write, not rates of what happened. Report them as “31 of 60 Build B comments mentioned being lost or unclear”, not as “52% of Build B players got lost”.

Quotes as evidence

Quote a comment to show a pattern, never to be the pattern. One vivid quote beside a chart of frustration quits makes the chart human. One vivid quote instead of the chart is an anecdote. Pick quotes that are typical of their code, say how many comments the code covers, and never quote only the comments that agree with you.

Triangulation

Triangulation means checking a finding against more than one source. Three outcomes are possible, and each tells you something different:

The sources… What it means What to write
Agree The finding is strong Lead with it, and cite every source
Disagree One source is measuring something different from what you thought Report both, and explain which to trust for which question
Only one source has anything The finding rests on one kind of evidence Report it with that caveat, and plan the missing source for the next test

Worked Example

The telemetry says Build B fixed navigation. Do the players agree? Put every source on the navigation question side by side.

Telemetry. Build A players averaged 8.9 wrong turns per session; Build B players 5.5. Large, and very unlikely to be chance (Comparing Groups).

Self-report, ratings. “The level was easy to navigate” averaged 3.2 in Build A and 3.23 in Build B: no change. Across sessions it does not correlate with wrong turns at all (Spearman r = 0.05). “I always knew what my objective was” did improve, from 2.13 to 3.08.

Self-report, comments. 31 Build B comments were coded lost or unclear, against 23 in Build A: the opposite of the telemetry. And 3 Build A players wrote that “the signposting helped”, about the build without the new signage. Split by enjoyment instead of build, the codes separate cleanly:

coded |> count(code, enjoyed = enjoyment >= 5)
# A tibble: 4 × 3
  code            enjoyed     n
  <chr>           <lgl>   <int>
1 Difficulty      FALSE      35
2 Found the way   TRUE       21
3 General         TRUE       10
4 Lost or unclear FALSE      54

Every found the way comment came from a player who rated enjoyment 5 or above, and every lost or unclear comment from one who rated it lower. The comments record how the session felt, not where the player went.

Observation. Tidebreak did not record any. That is the gap this example exposes.

Putting it together.

  • What happened: Build B cut wrong turns. Only telemetry measured it directly, and it is the source to trust for what happened.
  • What players noticed: not much. The navigation rating did not move, and players appear not to know when they are lost.
  • What the comments measure: mood. Useful for knowing how the session felt; weak evidence about navigation in either direction.
  • What is missing: someone watching the junctions, to see whether players hesitate and backtrack, and whether they seem aware of it.

What should the team do? Keep Build B’s guidance on the strength of the telemetry. Stop relying on the navigation rating to check navigation on this level. In the next playtest, add an observation sheet for the first two junctions, so the telemetry and the observation can be compared directly.

In the Discussion you would write: “The reduction in wrong turns was not reflected in players’ navigation ratings or comments. The comments tracked enjoyment rather than build, which suggests they describe how the session felt rather than where the player went. We treat the telemetry as the evidence for navigation, and recommend structured observation at the junctions in the next playtest to check whether players notice when they are lost.”

Writing Limitations

Every playtest has weaknesses. Naming them is not an admission of failure: it is what lets the reader trust everything else. A limitation you name costs you less than one the reader finds.

A good limitation is specific to your playtest, says what it might change, and says whether you checked it.

Kind Generic (weak) Specific (useful)
Sample “The sample was small.” “30 players; how they were recruited was not recorded, so we cannot say how closely they match the game’s audience.”
Design “There may be confounds.” “Each player played one build, so differences between the two groups of players could explain part of the gap. The cohort mix in each build is in Appendix C.”
Independence “Data may not be independent.” “Each player contributed 4 sessions. The result holds when each player counts once.”
Measurement “Surveys are subjective.” “The navigation rating did not track wrong turns, so it is not a reliable check on navigation for this level.”
Coding “Comments were interpreted.” “Comments were coded by keyword into four codes (Appendix B); a second coder was not used.”
Not measured “More research is needed.” “No observation was recorded, so we cannot say whether players noticed when they were lost.”

Limitations go in the Discussion, close to the claims they qualify. The Conclusion should already take them into account.

For your project: write your limitations as you go, not on the last night. Each time you make a decision you are not sure about (a row dropped, a code merged, a measure you could not collect), add a line.

Common Mistakes

Reporting only the source that tells the best story. The reader cannot see what you left out. Report every source you collected on the question.

Treating a count of comments as a rate. A comment is what a player chose to write. “Ten players mentioned the lighting” is not “ten players noticed the lighting”.

Coding without a codebook. If the reader cannot see what each code means, they cannot judge the counts.

Too many codes. Fifteen codes on sixty comments gives counts of two and three. Four to six is plenty.

One quote as the finding. A quote illustrates a pattern; it cannot be one.

Explaining away a disagreement. When telemetry and self-report disagree, the disagreement is a finding. Report it and say what each source is measuring.

Generic limitations. “The sample was small” tells the reader nothing they can use.

Practice

Each takes two or three minutes.

  1. Classify. Which source is each of these? (a) A death count. (b) “Player paused for 20 seconds at the bridge.” (c) A 1–7 enjoyment rating. (d) The time at which a player first opened the map. (e) “I didn’t know I could sprint.”
  2. Compute. Among frustration quits only, which comment code is most common?
  3. Critique. “12 of 60 players complained about the camera, so the camera is a problem for 20% of players.” What is wrong?
  4. Rewrite. Make this limitation specific: “There may be other factors that affected the results.”
NoteShow answers

1. (a) Telemetry. (b) Observation. (c) Self-report. (d) Telemetry. (e) Self-report.

2.

coded |>
  filter(quit_reason == "Frustrated") |>
  count(code, sort = TRUE)
# A tibble: 4 × 2
  code                n
  <chr>           <int>
1 Difficulty         12
2 Lost or unclear     9
3 Found the way       2
4 General             2

Difficulty, with 12, ahead of lost or unclear with 9. Yet the survey says frustration quitters did not know their objective. Players name the symptom they felt, not the cause, which is why comments illustrate a finding rather than measure it.

3. A comment is what a player chose to write, not a measurement. Players who did not mention the camera may still have had trouble with it, and players who mentioned it may have been mildly irritated. Report “12 of 60 comments mentioned the camera”, and check it against telemetry or observation, such as how often the camera was reset.

4. Name the factor, what it might change, and whether you checked it. For example: “Experienced players may navigate better whichever build they play. We checked the wrong-turns result within each cohort.” Any specific factor, checked or not, is better than “other factors”.

Summary

  • Telemetry says what happened, observation how it unfolded, self-report what players experienced. Choose measures from at least two for every question that matters.
  • Observation is evidence only when it is recorded the same way for every player: use an observation sheet with a fixed list of behaviours.
  • Code comments with a short, written codebook; apply it in R with case_when() and grepl(); check a sample by hand; report counts as counts of comments.
  • Quote to illustrate a pattern, never to be the pattern.
  • Triangulate. Agreement makes a finding strong; disagreement tells you what each source is really measuring.
  • Write limitations that are specific, say what they might change, and say whether you checked.
Key term Meaning
Triangulation Checking a finding against more than one source of evidence
Observation sheet A fixed list of behaviours recorded the same way for every player
Code A category given to a piece of free text
Codebook The list of codes, what each means, and an example of each
Limitation A specific weakness that could change the conclusion, stated by the author
