Why Developers Measure Anything

And what is actually worth measuring

Every team makes decisions about their game constantly. Most of those decisions are made from memory, taste and the loudest voice in the room, and most of the time that is fine — taste is why anyone plays your game rather than a spreadsheet.

Measurement earns its place in the cases where taste reliably fails. There are three of them, and they recur in every project.

You cannot see your own game any more. You have played your level five hundred times. You know where the key is. You cannot un-know it, and no amount of care will let you experience your own tutorial as a stranger. This is the single most common cause of a game that the team finds obvious and the player finds impenetrable.

You cannot feel a rare event. A stutter that happens once every two seconds occupies less than one per cent of frames. Your average frame rate will not show it. Your players will talk about nothing else.

You cannot remember twenty playtests. After a session you remember the two people who struggled most and the last person you watched. You do not remember the distribution, and you will confidently reconstruct one that never existed.

The Three Sources

Almost everything you will analyse in a game project comes from one of three places, and they have very different characters.

Telemetry is what the game records about itself while it runs: positions, events, deaths, times, item pickups. It is objective, it arrives in enormous quantities, and it tells you what happened and never why. Telemetry is cheap to collect and easy to collect badly — a log with no session identifier or no timestamp is close to worthless.

Performance capture is a special case of telemetry with its own conventions and its own failure modes. Frame times, memory, draw calls. It is the one area where the standard summary — average frame rate — is actively misleading, which is why Section 5 spends its time there.

Asking people is surveys, interviews and think-aloud sessions. It is the only source that tells you why, and the least reliable. People misremember, they are polite, they rationalise, and they answer the question they think you meant. A survey tells you what a player believes about their experience, which is a real and useful thing, but it is not the same as what happened.

The three disagree, and that is the point. The most valuable finding in any playtest is usually the place where telemetry and opinion contradict each other: players say the level is fine and the heat map shows nobody finds the side route; players say the game runs smoothly and the frame log shows a hitch every two seconds. A team with only one source never finds these.

Turning a Vague Question into a Measurable One

The question a designer asks is almost never answerable as asked.

What you are asked What you can actually measure
“Is the level too hard?” Deaths per attempt; time to first completion; proportion who never finish; where attempts end
“Does the game feel good?” Time to first successful interaction; retry rate after failure; rated responsiveness
“Is the tutorial working?” Proportion who use the taught mechanic later, unprompted
“Do people like the art?” Rated appeal; which screenshots they choose; where they stop to look

Notice that each right-hand cell names a specific number you could write down after watching one person play. That is the test. If you cannot describe how you would record it for a single player, you have not finished turning the question into a measurement.

Notice also that the right-hand column is often not one measure but several, and that they can disagree. This is not a failure of the method. A tutorial that everybody completes and nobody learns from is a real and common outcome, and only two measures together can find it.

Deciding in Advance

Before you collect anything, write down what result would change your mind. “If more than a third of testers never find the second key, I will add a light cue.”

This costs nothing and protects you from the most comfortable mistake in the discipline: running the test, getting an ambiguous result, and finding in it whatever you already believed. If the threshold is written down beforehand, the data gets to answer. If it is not, you will answer and attribute it to the data.

Reflective Questions

  • Pick a mechanic in your own project. Write the designer’s question, then three different measures that would bear on it, then say which one you would trust most and why.
  • Name something about your own game that you can no longer judge, because you have played it too often.
  • Your team disagrees about whether a puzzle is too obscure. What number, recorded from a single playtester, would settle it?
