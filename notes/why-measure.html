Why Developers Measure Anything

And what is actually worth measuring

The first notes page of the strand, for everyone, before Lab 01. It sets out when measurement is worth the effort, where playtest data comes from, and how to turn a designer’s question into something you can record. The only code is in the R Demonstrations section near the end.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of these notes you can:

  • name the three cases where a team’s judgement of its own game reliably fails;
  • distinguish telemetry, performance capture and asking people, and say what each can and cannot tell you;
  • rewrite a vague design question as a measure you could record for a single player;
  • write down, before collecting anything, the result that would change your mind;
  • load a playtest CSV into R and take a first look at it with read_csv(), glimpse() and summary().

Why This Matters for Playtesting

Every team makes decisions about their game constantly. Most of those decisions are made from memory, taste and the loudest voice in the room, and most of the time that is fine — taste is why anyone plays your game rather than a spreadsheet.

Measurement earns its place in the cases where taste reliably fails. There are three of them, and they recur in every project.

You cannot see your own game any more. You have played your level five hundred times. You know where the key is. You cannot un-know it, and no amount of care will let you experience your own tutorial as a stranger. This is the single most common cause of a game that the team finds obvious and the player finds impenetrable.

You cannot feel a rare event. A stutter that happens once every two seconds occupies less than one per cent of frames. Your average frame rate will not show it. Your players will talk about nothing else.

You cannot remember twenty playtests. After a session you remember the two people who struggled most and the last person you watched. You do not remember the distribution, and you will confidently reconstruct one that never existed.

The Three Sources

Almost everything you will analyse in a game project comes from one of three places, and they have very different characters.

Telemetry is what the game records about itself while it runs: positions, events, deaths, times, item pickups. It is objective, it arrives in enormous quantities, and it tells you what happened and never why. Telemetry is cheap to collect and easy to collect badly — a log with no session identifier or no timestamp is close to worthless.

Performance capture is a special case of telemetry with its own conventions and its own failure modes. Frame times, memory, draw calls. It is the one area where the standard summary — average frame rate — is actively misleading, which is why Section 5 spends its time there.

Asking people is surveys, interviews and think-aloud sessions. It is the only source that tells you why, and the least reliable. People misremember, they are polite, they rationalise, and they answer the question they think you meant. A survey tells you what a player believes about their experience, which is a real and useful thing, but it is not the same as what happened.

The three disagree, and that is the point. The most valuable finding in any playtest is usually the place where telemetry and opinion contradict each other: players say the level is fine and the heat map shows nobody finds the side route; players say the game runs smoothly and the frame log shows a hitch every two seconds. A team with only one source never finds these.

Turning a Vague Question into a Measurable One

The question a designer asks is almost never answerable as asked.

What you are asked What you can actually measure
“Is the level too hard?” Deaths per attempt; time to first completion; proportion who never finish; where attempts end
“Does the game feel good?” Time to first successful interaction; retry rate after failure; rated responsiveness
“Is the tutorial working?” Proportion who use the taught mechanic later, unprompted
“Do people like the art?” Rated appeal; which screenshots they choose; where they stop to look

Notice that each right-hand cell names a specific number you could write down after watching one person play. That is the test. If you cannot describe how you would record it for a single player, you have not finished turning the question into a measurement.

Notice also that the right-hand column is often not one measure but several, and that they can disagree. This is not a failure of the method. A tutorial that everybody completes and nobody learns from is a real and common outcome, and only two measures together can find it.

Deciding in Advance

Before you collect anything, write down what result would change your mind. “If more than a third of testers never find the second key, I will add a light cue.”

This costs nothing and protects you from the most comfortable mistake in the discipline: running the test, getting an ambiguous result, and finding in it whatever you already believed. If the threshold is written down beforehand, the data gets to answer. If it is not, you will answer and attribute it to the data.

Worked Examples

The question as asked. The Tidebreak team ran 120 playtest sessions, 60 on Build A and 60 on Build B. The designer’s question is “is Build A worse?”, which cannot be answered as asked: worse at what, for whom, by how much?

The question as measured. Two measures bear on it, one from each end of the three sources:

Source Measure Recorded per player as
Telemetry Did the session end in a frustration quit? quit_reason is Frustrated
Asking people “I always knew what my objective was” A rating from 1 to 5

The threshold, written first. Before anyone opens the file, the team writes: “If more than a quarter of the sessions on either build end in a frustration quit, that build does not ship.”

The result. 20 of 60 Build A sessions ended in a frustration quit (33%), against 5 of 60 on Build B (8%). Build A crosses the line; Build B does not. Nobody has to argue about it, because the argument happened before the data arrived.

The second source. Of the 8 survey items, the objective item is the lowest rated, with a mean of 2.61 on the 1 to 5 scale. Telemetry says what happened: players on Build A left in frustration. The survey offers a candidate for why: players did not know what they were meant to be doing. Neither source alone would have pointed a designer at the objective marker.

summary() gives the minimum, quartiles, median, mean and maximum of every numeric column. Read down the Mean rows and the weakest survey item stands out without any further code.

sum(sessions$build == "A" & sessions$quit_reason == "Frustrated")
[1] 20
sum(sessions$build == "B" & sessions$quit_reason == "Frustrated")
[1] 5

A condition such as sessions$build == "A" produces one TRUE or FALSE per row, and sum() counts the TRUEs. Combining two conditions with & counts the rows where both hold. This is how every number in the worked example was produced.

Interpretation Guidance

Every finding is read at three levels, and the discipline is keeping them apart.

What the data shows. 20 of 60 Build A sessions ended in a frustration quit, against 5 of 60 on Build B. This is a count. It is not in dispute.

What the analysis suggests. Something in Build A frustrates players that Build B avoided, and the survey’s weakest item points at objective clarity as a candidate.

What can legitimately be concluded. Build A fails the threshold the team set in advance. That is all. The survey figure is averaged across both builds, so on its own it cannot say that the unclear objective belongs to Build A. Linking the two is a hypothesis for the next test, not a finding.

Telemetry reports behaviour and surveys report belief. When they agree, you have a lead. When they disagree, you have the most valuable finding in the playtest, and the disagreement itself is what goes in the report.

Common Mistakes

Collecting first, asking later. Logging everything and hoping a question emerges produces large files and no decisions. Write the question down first (rule 2).

Trusting one source. A survey on its own tells you what players believe; a log on its own tells you what they did. Treat either alone as half the evidence.

Setting the threshold after seeing the result. A threshold chosen after the data has been seen is always met by exactly the right margin. If it was not written down beforehand, it is not a threshold (rule 1).

Logging without identifiers. A death event with no session or player ID cannot be joined to anything, and a survey response with no session ID cannot be joined to the telemetry from the same match.

Reading glimpse() and moving on. The types it prints are R’s guess. A 1 to 5 rating arrives as <dbl>, and R will average it without complaint. Whether it should be averaged is a decision R cannot make for you.

Short Practice Activities

Each takes two or three minutes.

1. Run glimpse(survey). How many survey items are there, and what type has R given them?

NoteCheck your answer

There are 8 items after session_id, and every one is <dbl>. R treats them as ordinary numbers; they are ordinal ratings, and the difference matters as soon as you average them.

2. Count the sessions that ended with quit_reason equal to "Technical fault", then write the threshold that would make you delay a release over it.

NoteCheck your answer 
sum(sessions$quit_reason == "Technical fault")
[1] 5

Any threshold is defensible if it was written first and names a number: “if more than one session in twenty ends in a technical fault, we do not release”. “If it seems like a lot” is not a threshold.

3. Rewrite “is the corridor too dangerous?” as one telemetry measure and one survey measure, each recordable for a single player.

NoteCheck your answer

Telemetry: deaths in the corridor per session. Asking people: agreement with “I understood why I died”. Either alone is weaker than both together: players who die there often and do not understand why describe a different problem from players who die there often and accept it.

Summary

  • Measurement earns its place where taste fails: you cannot see your own game, feel a rare event, or remember twenty playtests.
  • Telemetry says what happened, performance capture says how the machine coped, and asking people says what players believe. The disagreements are the findings.
  • A question is measurable when you can say how you would record it for one player.
  • Write the threshold that would change your mind before you look.
  • read_csv(), glimpse() and summary() are the first three things you run on any new file.

Reflective Questions

  • Pick a mechanic in your own project. Write the designer’s question, then three different measures that would bear on it, then say which one you would trust most and why.
  • Name something about your own game that you can no longer judge, because you have played it too often.
  • Your team disagrees about whether a puzzle is too obscure. What number, recorded from a single playtester, would settle it?
