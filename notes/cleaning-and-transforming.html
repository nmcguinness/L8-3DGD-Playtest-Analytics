Cleaning and Transforming

Every cleaning choice is part of the analysis

Week 2, first cycle, for everyone, before Part A of Lab 02. It covers getting a raw export into a shape that can answer a question: finding what is missing, doubled, mislabelled or impossible, and deciding what to do about each. Then it introduces the six dplyr verbs and the pipe, and shows what they are doing by setting them next to the base R you already know. The worked example is the one to remember: eight blank cells that were hiding the finding.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of these notes you can:

  • say what an NA means in a playtest export, and check whether the pattern of missing values is itself a finding;
  • find exact duplicates and duplicates on the key, and remove them;
  • tell an impossible value from an unusual one, and treat each correctly;
  • repair inconsistent category labels and convert text to numbers without losing data silently;
  • keep a cleaning log that records every row dropped and what it did to n;
  • use select(), filter(), mutate(), arrange(), group_by() and summarise(), composed with the pipe;
  • explain what group_by() + summarise() does by writing the same answer with tapply().

Why This Matters for Playtesting

No playtest export arrives ready. Telemetry drops events when a build crashes. Spreadsheets get pasted into twice. Someone types “veteran” where the form said “Veteran”. Every one of these has to be dealt with before any summary means anything, and every way of dealing with it is a choice.

Cleaning choices change conclusions. Dropping the rows with a blank cell is one line of R. If the blanks are not scattered at random, that line removes a particular kind of player, and the summary you compute afterwards describes the players who were left. The worked example below does exactly this to the strongest finding in the Tidebreak data.

That is why cleaning goes in the report. A reader who cannot see what you removed cannot judge what you found. Cleaning is analysis (rule 6): record every choice where the reader can see it, not hidden in a script.

What NA Actually Means

NA is R’s marker for a missing value: a cell with nothing in it. It does not mean zero, and it does not mean the event did not happen. It means the export does not know. A blank deaths cell is not a player who never died.

NA is contagious. Any calculation that touches an NA returns NA, because R will not guess what the missing value was. mean(deaths) on a column with one blank returns NA. Adding na.rm = TRUE tells R to leave the blanks out, which is itself a decision: it assumes the missing players look like the ones you can see.

Three questions, in this order, before you do anything to a missing value:

  1. How much is missing? A handful of blanks in 120 rows is different from half a column.
  2. Where is it missing? Split the count by build, cohort and outcome. If the blanks cluster, something produced them.
  3. Is the pattern the finding? A build whose quit path never writes the session length has a telemetry bug, and the players on that path are the ones the playtest was run to find (rule 9).

Duplicates

A duplicate is a row that appears more than once. It inflates n, and it counts one player’s experience twice.

An exact duplicate repeats every column. It is almost always an export or copy-paste fault, and removing it is safe. A duplicate on the key repeats the identifier, here session_id, but may differ elsewhere. That is two records claiming to be the same session, and you have to work out which one is right before you remove either.

Check both. If the two counts match, every key duplicate is an exact copy. If they do not, look at the rows that differ before deciding.

Impossible Versus Unusual

An impossible value cannot happen in the game: a negative session length, a rating of 9 on a 1–7 scale, more objectives captured than the map has. It is an error. Fix it if you can trace the cause, otherwise set it to NA, and log it either way.

An unusual value is extreme but possible: a player who never died, a Sentinel who scored almost nothing. It is data. Removing it because it spoils a chart is deleting the players you most need to hear from.

The test is the game’s rules, not the chart. A boxplot flags points by distance from the rest. Only you know whether the game allows that value. Write the rule down, such as session_minutes > 0 or enjoyment in 1–7, then check the data against it.

Labels and Types

Inconsistent labels. R compares text exactly. "Veteran", "veteran" and "Veteran " with a trailing space are three different cohorts to table(), and a grouped summary will report three groups where the design had one. trimws() strips leading and trailing spaces and tolower() makes the case consistent. Use them together, and always check the result with table(). read_csv() trims spaces on import by default, which hides them in R but not in the file anyone else opens.

Numbers stored as text. When one cell in a numeric column contains "n/a" or a comma decimal, read_csv() reads the whole column as text. as.numeric() converts it back, and turns every value it cannot read into NA with only a warning. Count the NAs before and after converting: any increase is data you just lost.

Factors. A factor is a categorical variable that knows its possible values and their order. factor(cohort, levels = c("novice", "regular", "veteran")) fixes the order to the order of experience, so tables and plots read left to right from least to most experienced, not alphabetically. It also makes a misspelled label visible: anything not in levels becomes NA rather than a fourth group (rule 3).

Keeping a Cleaning Log

A cleaning log is a list of every change made to the data, in order, with the number of rows before and after. It is short, and it goes in the report.

Step Rows after Why
Raw export 124 As delivered
Remove exact duplicates 120 4 sessions exported twice
Standardise cohort labels 120 18 values re-spaced or re-cased; no rows removed
Keep blank session_minutes 120 All 8 are Build A frustration quits; see the worked example

A step that removes no rows still goes in the log. Relabelling changes which group a player is counted in, and the reader needs to know that happened.

The Six Verbs and the Pipe

The dplyr package, part of the tidyverse, reduces most data manipulation to six verbs. Each takes a data frame and returns a data frame.

Verb What it does Base R you already know
select() Keep some columns sessions[, c("build", "score")]
filter() Keep some rows sessions[sessions$build == "A", ]
mutate() Add or change a column sessions$kd <- sessions$kills / sessions$deaths
arrange() Sort the rows sessions[order(sessions$score), ]
group_by() Mark the groups The second argument of tapply()
summarise() One row of results per group The function inside tapply()

The pipe, |>, passes the result on its left in as the first argument of the function on its right. sessions |> filter(build == "A") means filter(sessions, build == "A"). Its value is in chaining: a sequence of verbs reads top to bottom, in the order the steps happen, instead of inside out.

You will also see %>%. It is the older tidyverse pipe and does the same job in everything you will write here. Distributions and Variation used it; these notes use |>, which is built into R and needs no package.

Worked Examples

Which build keeps players longer? The raw export has 124 rows. After removing the 4 duplicates, 8 sessions have a blank session_minutes. Every one of them is Build A. Every one of them ended in a frustration quit.

table(build   = deduped$build,
      missing = is.na(deduped$session_minutes))
     missing
build FALSE TRUE
    A    52    8
    B    60    0

That pattern is not random, so both obvious ways of handling it are wrong.

Choice 1: drop incomplete rows. complete.cases() keeps only rows with no blank anywhere. It keeps 109 of 120 sessions. Before, Build A had 20 frustration quits against Build B’s 5. After, it has 12 against 5. One line of cleaning removed 40% of the evidence for Build A’s biggest problem, and nothing warned you.

Choice 2: leave them out of the median. With na.rm = TRUE, Build A’s median session is 26.1 minutes. But the Build A frustration quits that were recorded have a median of 11.2 minutes. The missing sessions are almost certainly short ones, so leaving them out makes Build A look better than it is.

What the complete data says. For this exercise the unbroken export exists, so the damage can be measured. Build A’s true median is 23.2 minutes, not 26.1. The blank cells were never noise. They were the shortest, angriest sessions in the playtest, lost by a telemetry bug on exactly the path the playtest was meant to examine.

The defensible handling keeps the rows, counts them from quit_reason, which survived, reports the median with a note that 8 short sessions are missing from it, and files a bug against Build A’s session logging.

The six verbs

sessions |>
  filter(build == "A", quit_reason == "Frustrated") |>
  mutate(logged = !is.na(session_minutes)) |>
  select(session_id, cohort, session_minutes, logged) |>
  arrange(session_minutes) |>
  head(4)
# A tibble: 4 × 4
  session_id cohort  session_minutes logged
  <chr>      <fct>             <dbl> <lgl> 
1 S117       regular             8.6 TRUE  
2 S043       novice              9.3 TRUE  
3 S097       novice              9.6 TRUE  
4 S039       regular            10.3 TRUE

Read it as a sentence: take the sessions, keep Build A’s frustration quits, add a column saying whether the length was logged, keep four columns, sort by length, show the first four. Each line is one step, and removing a line removes one step. arrange() puts NA last, so the shortest recorded quits come first.

The same question, twice

What is the average number of wrong turns in each build?

tapply(sessions$wrong_turns,
       sessions$build,
       mean)
sessions |>
  group_by(build) |>
  summarise(mean_wrong = mean(wrong_turns))
       A        B 
8.916667 5.516667 
# A tibble: 2 × 2
  build mean_wrong
  <chr>      <dbl>
1 A           8.92
2 B           5.52

Same numbers: the tibble prints three significant figures, but stores the same values. Both do the same three things, which together are called split–apply–combine: split wrong_turns into one piece per build, apply mean() to each piece, combine the results. tapply() takes all three as arguments in one call and returns a named vector. group_by() does the split, summarise() does the apply, and the combine is a tibble with one row per group.

The difference shows when the question grows. How many sessions does each build have, how many are missing a length, and what is the median length of the rest?

cbind(
  n       = tapply(sessions$session_minutes,
                   sessions$build, length),
  missing = tapply(is.na(sessions$session_minutes),
                   sessions$build, sum),
  median  = tapply(sessions$session_minutes,
                   sessions$build, median,
                   na.rm = TRUE)
)
sessions |>
  group_by(build) |>
  summarise(
    n       = n(),
    missing = sum(is.na(session_minutes)),
    median  = median(session_minutes,
                     na.rm = TRUE)
  )
   n missing median
A 60       8  26.15
B 60       0  27.10
# A tibble: 2 × 4
  build     n missing median
  <chr> <int>   <int>  <dbl>
1 A        60       8   26.2
2 B        60       0   27.1

tapply() needs one call per statistic, each repeating the column and the grouping, and cbind() to stick them together. summarise() takes as many statistics as you like against one group_by(). The answer is the worked example in one table: all 8 missing lengths are Build A’s, and the median beside them is computed without them.

Interpretation Guidance

What the data shows. The raw export has 4 duplicated sessions, 18 mislabelled cohorts and 8 blank session lengths, all in Build A frustration quits.

What the analysis suggests. Build A’s quit path does not write the session length. The blanks are a telemetry fault concentrated in the sessions that matter most.

What can legitimately be concluded. Build A’s frustration-quit count is reliable, because it comes from quit_reason, which is complete. Its median session length is an overestimate by an amount you cannot know from this export. Say both.

Report what you did, not only what you found. The cleaning log goes in the report’s experimental design section. A reader should be able to reproduce your n from it.

Common Mistakes

Dropping incomplete rows by reflex. na.omit() and complete.cases() are one line each and can remove the finding. Check where the blanks are first.

Treating NA as zero. A blank deaths cell is unknown, not a player who never died. Replacing it with 0 invents a very good player.

Using na.rm = TRUE without saying so. It is the right tool, and it changes what the number describes. Report how many values it left out.

Fixing labels without checking. Run table() before and after. A label you did not know about survives tolower().

Deleting unusual values as if they were impossible. The game’s rules decide what is impossible. The chart does not.

Cleaning in a spreadsheet. Edits made by hand cannot be seen, repeated or reviewed. Every change belongs in code, where it runs again on the next export.

Short Practice Activities

Each takes two or three minutes. Start from sessions, the de-duplicated data with fixed labels from the demonstrations.

1. Which rows would complete.cases() remove, split by build and quit reason?

NoteCheck your answer 
dropped <- sessions[!complete.cases(sessions), ]
table(dropped$build, dropped$quit_reason)
   
    Completed test Frustrated Lost interest
  A              2          8             0
  B              0          0             1

11 rows. The 8 Build A frustration quits are the blank lengths; the other 3 are blank deaths cells scattered across other outcomes. Those two groups need different handling, and complete.cases() treats them the same.

2. Rewrite tapply(sessions$score, sessions$archetype, median) with group_by() and summarise(), and add the number of sessions per archetype.

NoteCheck your answer 
sessions |>
  group_by(archetype) |>
  summarise(n = n(), median_score = median(score))
# A tibble: 4 × 3
  archetype     n median_score
  <chr>     <int>        <dbl>
1 Duelist      30        1426.
2 Engineer     30        1472.
3 Scout        30        1546 
4 Sentinel     30        1528.

Adding n = n() costs one argument in summarise() and a second tapply() call in base R. Always report n next to a group summary (rule 8).

3. Using the pipe, list the sessions with a blank deaths cell, showing only session_id, build and quit_reason.

NoteCheck your answer 
sessions |>
  filter(is.na(deaths)) |>
  select(session_id, build, quit_reason)
# A tibble: 3 × 3
  session_id build quit_reason   
  <chr>      <chr> <chr>         
1 S022       B     Lost interest 
2 S025       A     Completed test
3 S077       A     Completed test

No pattern across builds or outcomes: these blanks look scattered, so leaving them out of a deaths summary with na.rm = TRUE is defensible. That is a conclusion about these three rows, not a rule for every blank.

Summary

  • NA means unknown. Count it, find where it is, and ask whether the pattern is the finding before doing anything to it.
  • Check duplicates both exactly and on the key.
  • Impossible values are errors; unusual values are data. The game’s rules decide which is which.
  • trimws() and tolower() fix labels; factor() fixes their order; table() checks the result.
  • Keep a cleaning log with n at every step. It goes in the report.
  • The six verbs, chained with |>, are split–apply–combine made readable. tapply() does the same thing in one call.
  • In Tidebreak, the 8 blank cells were the frustration quits. Dropping them would have hidden Build A’s worst problem.

Reflective Questions

  • A colleague removes every row with a blank cell “to be safe”. What would you need to know about those rows before you agreed with them?
  • Build A’s median session length is wrong in a direction you know but by an amount you do not. Should the report quote it at all? What would you write instead?
  • Two rows share a session_id but have different scores. What evidence would you use to decide which one to keep, and what do you do if there is none?
  • tapply() and group_by() give the same answer. When would you choose the base R version in your own analysis?
