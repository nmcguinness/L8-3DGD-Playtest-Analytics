Start Here

How the course works, and the route through it

Workflow
  1. Question
  2. Measure
  3. Collect
  4. Clean
  5. Describe
  6. Compare/Relate
  7. Interpret
  8. Act

This course teaches you to use playtest evidence to make better design decisions. You will plan a playtest, describe what happened, decide whether a change worked, and write it up as a finding your team can act on. It assumes no statistics and no R. It does assume you build games. Read this page first; it takes five minutes.

What You Are Learning

You are not training to be a statistician. You are learning to answer four questions about your own game, with evidence instead of opinion:

Question What you do Where you learn it
What happened? Clean the data, describe it, look at it Stage 1
Is the pattern credible? Compare builds or groups; relate one measure to another Stages 2 and 3
What does it mean for the game? Interpret the result in terms of the player’s experience every stage
What should we do next? Recommend a change, a fix, or the next test every stage, written up in Stage 4

The strip at the top of every notes page is the same idea as a process. Start with a question, decide what to measure, collect it, clean it, describe it, compare or relate, interpret, act. The highlighted steps are the ones that page covers.

Start with the question. Every technique in these notes is here because it answers a question a design team actually asks. If you cannot say what question a number answers, you do not need the number yet (rule 2).

The Four Stages

The course runs from October to March, alongside your group project.

Stage Question When What it gives you
1 Foundations What happened? October, one 3-hour class Plan a playtest, read a dataset into R, describe it, plot it
2 Comparing Evidence Did the change work? October, one 3-hour class Clean the raw data, compare two builds, judge whether the difference is real and whether it matters
3 Relationships & Visual Evidence What else does the data tell us? November to February, short clinics Report-quality figures with ggplot2; how measures relate to each other, on your own project’s data
4 Evidence to Decision What should we do, and how do we report it? March, one workshop Combine your evidence into findings and recommendations, written up in Quarto

Stages 1 and 2 use Tidebreak, a playtest dataset built for teaching. By Stage 3 your team will have its own playtest data, and the clinics work on that.

For your project: the final assessment report has the headings Executive Summary, Introduction, Methodology, Results, Discussion, Conclusion, References and Appendices. Everything in Stages 1–3 produces something that goes under one of them. Stage 4 puts it together.

The Route

Stages 1 and 2 are two 90-minute cycles each: a framing question, about 30 minutes of theory from the notes, a 15-minute demo, then the lab. The notes hold more than the theory slot covers. In class we cover each page’s core; the rest is there for when you need it.

When Notes Lab Demo
Before Stage 1 R from First Principles, with R open Lab 00
Stage 1, cycle 1 Planning a Playtest, Data, Variables and Types Lab 01 Part A Stage 1
Stage 1, cycle 2 Describing Variables, Distributions and Shape Lab 01 Part B Stage 1
Stage 2, cycle 1 Cleaning and Transforming Lab 02 Part A Stage 2
Stage 2, cycle 2 From Sample to Population, Comparing Groups Lab 02 Part B Stage 2
Stage 3 clinics Visualising with ggplot2, Relationships and Correlation Lab 03, Lab 04 Stage 3
Stage 4 workshop Interpreting and Reporting Evidence, Combining Evidence Lab 05 Stage 4

The R you will write. You handle data with the tidyverse: read_csv() to load it, then filter(), select(), group_by() and summarise() joined with the pipe, |>. In Stages 1 and 2 you plot with base R (hist(), boxplot(), plot()), which is quick and good for looking at data while you think. Stage 3 introduces ggplot2, for figures other people will read. There is one way to do each job. Where you will meet other ways in code you find online, the notes say so, and you only need to recognise them.

How a Notes Page Works

Every notes page has the same shape, so you always know where to look.

Part What it is for
Opening quote What the page is for, and the playtest question running through it
At a glance The stage, the time it takes, what to read first, key terms, the R you will write
Why This Matters Why a games team needs the idea at all
Core sections One idea each: what it is and why you need it, a figure, the Tidebreak example, the R, how you would say it in a report, and the common mistake
Worked Example The page’s question answered step by step, often overturning the obvious answer
Interpretation Guidance What the data shows, what it suggests, and what you can legitimately conclude
Common Mistakes The errors that turn up in real reports
Practice A few short questions, with the answers in one collapsed box underneath
Summary and Key Terms The page in a few lines, and a glossary for revision

Not everything is core. Sections are marked when they are not:

  • No label: core. You need to understand this and be able to use it.
  • Useful when needed: depends on your game and your data. Read it when your project needs it.
  • Going further: depth and background. Interesting, sometimes useful, never required. Collapsed boxes titled “Going further” and “The maths, if you want it” are the same.

Every number is computed. Each number in the prose is calculated from the data when the page is built, so it cannot be out of date or mistyped. Figures stamped Illustration use simulated data to show an idea cleanly; everything else is Tidebreak.

Type the code. Every chunk runs as shown, provided the data sits in the data/ folder of your project. Typing it, rather than copying it, is how it sticks.

The Labs and Reference Pages

The labs are where you do the analysis. Each exercise is tagged by what it asks of you:

Tier What it asks Time
[Do] One function, one result 2–3 minutes
[Apply] Combine two or three ideas 5 minutes
[Decide] A judgement call with no single right answer 5–8 minutes

Every exercise ends by asking you to write a sentence or two about what the result means. That is the part that matters. Anyone can run a t-test; the course is about knowing what the result means for the game. Solutions are collapsed under each exercise. Open them after you have tried, even when your attempt worked.

The reference pages are for looking things up during a lab or in your project:

  • Statistics Reference: every term in plain English, with an example and the common mistake
  • R and Quarto Reference: every function, by task
  • ggplot2 Reference: the figures you need from Stage 3

The Ten Rules are ten things to check every time you read playtest data. Pages and labs point to them by number: “rule 5 again” means that page.

The Game: Tidebreak

Tidebreak is a fictional four-player top-down arena game. Two builds were playtested: Build B added signposting to help players find their way. The playtest produced the files every page uses:

File One row is Used for
tidebreak_sessions.csv one player in one session almost everything
tidebreak_sessions_raw.csv the same, as exported, with faults cleaning
tidebreak_survey.csv one post-session survey ratings and reporting
tidebreak_paired.csv one player who played both builds the paired comparison
tidebreak_frame_times.csv one rendered frame performance

The data is synthetic, but it was built so that a careful analysis finds real findings, and several of them contradict the obvious first answer. That is the point. If a result surprises you, you are probably reading the data correctly.

Before Stage 1

  1. Work through Lab 00: Setting Up, and check that the test document renders.
  2. Read R from First Principles with R open beside you.
  3. Skim the Ten Rules. You will meet each of them properly later.

Then start with Planning a Playtest.
