Lab 01 — Reading Data

Stage 1: vectors, missing values, loading a playtest, and describing what happened

NoteGet the files
  • Data: tidebreak_sessions.csv. Save them in the data/ folder of your project.

Overview

This lab is your first hands-on session with R. You are not expected to have written a line of it before, but read R from First Principles first: every idea in Part A is introduced there.

The work is deliberately unglamorous. Before you can find anything interesting in a playtest you have to be able to load the data, look at it, and describe it without making a fool of yourself. That is what this lab trains. By the end you will have answered the Stage 1 question, what happened?, for a real playtest, and said what it means for the game.

Function What it does
c(), length(), class() Build a vector; how many values, and what type
is.na(), na.rm = TRUE Find missing values; leave them out of a calculation
read_csv(), glimpse() Read a CSV; look at it without printing every row
$ Pull out one column as a vector
count() Count each value of a categorical variable
mean(), median(), sd(), IQR(), quantile() Describe a numeric variable
group_by(), summarise(), n(), \|> The same summaries, once per group
hist(), boxplot() See the shape of a variable

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lab you can:

  • create and index vectors, and explain what NA does to a calculation
  • load a CSV and inspect it without printing 120 rows
  • classify any column as nominal, ordinal, discrete or continuous
  • describe a numeric variable with an appropriate centre and spread, and draw it
  • summarise a variable for each group with group_by() and summarise()
  • read a retention curve, and say in one sentence when the mean is the wrong summary and why

Ground rules

  • Keep the CSV files in the data/ folder of the project.
  • Type the code. Do not copy and paste from the solutions until you have tried.
  • Open the solution after your attempt, even when your attempt worked. The solution sometimes does it a better way.
  • Every exercise ends with a short written answer. Write it. The writing is the part that matters; the code is how you get there.
  • If a line of code fails, read the error before doing anything else. Most R errors name the exact problem.

Exercise 03 — A missing value poisons the arithmetic [Do]

A seventh tester’s death count was not recorded. Add NA to the end of deaths and take the mean. Then take it again so that the result is a number.

NoteShow solution 
deaths <- c(deaths, NA)

mean(deaths)
[1] NA
mean(deaths, na.rm = TRUE)
[1] 3.5

NA is not zero and it is not “empty”. It means unknown, and the mean of a set containing an unknown value is itself unknown. R tells you that rather than quietly guessing.

Write 2 sentences: na.rm = TRUE makes the NA go away. Explain why that is not the same thing as fixing the problem.

Exercise 04 — Count with a condition [Apply]

Using the seven-value deaths vector, produce a TRUE/FALSE vector showing which sessions had more than 3 deaths, use it to extract those death counts, then count how many sessions that is and what proportion of the recorded sessions it is.

NoteShow solution 
deaths > 3
[1]  TRUE FALSE  TRUE FALSE FALSE  TRUE    NA
deaths[deaths > 3]
[1]  4  7  5 NA
sum(deaths > 3, na.rm = TRUE)
[1] 3
mean(deaths > 3, na.rm = TRUE)
[1] 0.5

Two things worth noticing. deaths[deaths > 3] keeps the NA, because R does not know whether the unknown value is above 3 or not. And sum() on a TRUE/FALSE vector counts the TRUEs, because TRUE is 1 and FALSE is 0, so mean() gives the proportion.

Write 1–2 sentences: why is sum(x > 3) a count rather than a total?

Exercise 09 — “Only one in five” [Decide]

The producer counts the quit reasons and says: “Only one in five players quit in frustration. Most players are fine, so frustration is not a priority.” Count quit_reason, then find the share of frustration quits in each build, and decide whether the producer’s reading is defensible.

NoteShow solution 
sessions |> count(quit_reason)
# A tibble: 4 × 2
  quit_reason         n
  <chr>           <int>
1 Completed test     69
2 Frustrated         25
3 Lost interest      21
4 Technical fault     5
sessions |>
  group_by(build) |>
  summarise(n = n(),
            frustrated = mean(quit_reason == "Frustrated"))
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  build     n frustrated
  <chr> <int>      <dbl>
1 A        60     0.333 
2 B        60     0.0833

One defensible answer: no, because “one in five” averages two different games. Overall, 25 of 120 sessions ended in frustration, 21%. Split by build, that is 20 of 60 in Build A (33%) and 5 of 60 in Build B (8%). One build loses a third of its players to frustration; the other hardly any. The overall figure describes neither.

What makes it defensible. It breaks the count down by the one thing the playtest was designed to compare before judging it, and it reads the numbers as a difference between builds rather than a verdict on the game.

What would make it indefensible. Accepting “one in five” because it is smaller than the number who completed the test, without asking where the one in five came from. Or going the other way and calling the whole game frustrating, when Build B barely is.

Write 2 sentences: what would you say to the producer instead, and what would you want to check next?

  <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
1  22.8   26.2  7.76  13.8

Both ways work. The first is quick for one variable; summarise() puts every summary in one table, which is what you want once there is more than one. quantile() with no second argument gives the five-number summary.

Write 2 sentences: express the gap between mean and median as a percentage of the median, for both variables. Which one is more skewed, and does its tail run left or right?

NoteShow answer

Score’s mean sits 2.8% below its median. Session minutes sits 12.9% below. Both are left-skewed: the mean falls below the median because a group of low values drags it down. But session minutes is far more so.

Comparing the raw gaps (-41 points against -3.4 minutes) would have told you the opposite, which is why the scale of the variable always has to come into it.

Exercise 14 — When do we lose them? [Decide]

The producer has the two mean session lengths, 21.3 minutes for Build A and 24.2 for Build B, and says: “Build A sessions are about three minutes shorter. Players drift off a bit sooner, that’s all.”

A retention curve shows the share of sessions still going at every minute. Run this code to draw one for each build; you do not need to be able to write it. Then decide whether “drift off a bit sooner” is a fair description.

mins_a <- sessions$session_minutes[sessions$build == "A"]
mins_b <- sessions$session_minutes[sessions$build == "B"]

minute  <- 0:32
still_a <- sapply(minute, function(m) mean(mins_a >= m))
still_b <- sapply(minute, function(m) mean(mins_b >= m))

plot(minute, still_a, type = "s", lwd = 2, col = "grey40", ylim = c(0, 1),
     main = "Share of sessions still playing",
     xlab = "Minute", ylab = "Share still playing")
lines(minute, still_b, type = "s", lwd = 2, col = "firebrick")
legend("bottomleft", legend = c("Build A", "Build B"), lwd = 2,
       col = c("grey40", "firebrick"), bty = "n")

mean(mins_a >= m) is the share of Build A sessions that lasted at least m minutes, the proportion trick again; sapply() repeats it for every minute.

NoteShow solution

One defensible answer: no. Build A does not drift; it drops. Between minutes 11 and 13, Build A loses 20% of its sessions; Build B loses 7% over the same two minutes. Something in the first dozen minutes of Build A drives a block of players out at once, and Exercise 09 already showed those quits are frustration. The investigation should start at minute 11 of Build A: watch the replays, check where those players were.

What makes it defensible. It looks at when players left, not just the average, and names a specific moment someone can investigate.

What would make it indefensible. Comparing the two means, agreeing that the difference is “a bit”, and moving on. The mean folds a sharp exit at minute 12 and a long tail of full sessions into one number that describes neither (rule 9).

Write 2 sentences: what would you ask the level designer to check first, and what number would tell you, in the next playtest, that the fix worked?

Challenge — Which cohort is the game actually built for? [Decide]

You have been asked a real question by the design lead: is Tidebreak too hard for novice players?

Using only what is in this lab, produce the evidence you would take into that conversation. There is no single correct answer. There is a defensible one and an indefensible one.

NoteShow solution 
# One table: size, score, deaths, session length and frustration, per cohort
sessions |>
  group_by(cohort) |>
  summarise(n              = n(),
            mean_score     = round(mean(score)),
            mean_deaths    = round(mean(deaths), 2),
            median_minutes = median(session_minutes),
            frustrated     = round(mean(quit_reason == "Frustrated"), 2))
# A tibble: 3 × 6
  cohort      n mean_score mean_deaths median_minutes frustrated
  <chr>   <int>      <dbl>       <dbl>          <dbl>      <dbl>
1 Novice     33       1253        3.24           27.1       0.18
2 Regular    53       1414        3.09           24.2       0.26
3 Veteran    34       1622        2.65           24.8       0.15
# Show the shape, not just the numbers
boxplot(score ~ cohort, data = sessions,
        main = "Score by player cohort", ylab = "Score", col = "grey85")

What the evidence shows. Novices do score lower, and that is the finding everyone expects. On its own it looks like a difficulty problem.

Every other measure contradicts it. Novices die at almost exactly the same rate as everyone else, they play for longer than either other cohort, and they are less likely to quit in frustration than regular players. A game that was too hard for novices would show them dying more and leaving sooner. This one does not.

So the defensible answer is: no. The score gap looks like inexperience rather than difficulty: novices are playing the game safely and slowly and scoring less for it. The genuinely odd number in that table is the frustration rate among regular players, which nobody asked about and which is the thing worth chasing next.

What would make it indefensible. Comparing three mean scores, announcing that novices are struggling, and recommending a difficulty change. The number is real and the conclusion does not follow from it.

Write 3–4 sentences, using What → Pattern → Meaning → Action:

  • What did you measure?
  • Pattern: what does the evidence show?
  • Meaning: what might that mean for the design?
  • Action: what is the one change or the one further test you would recommend?

Going further: Distributions and Shape says a normal distribution puts about 68% of values within 1 SD of the mean. If you want to check that against real columns, try the exercise at the end of Is It Normal Enough?.
