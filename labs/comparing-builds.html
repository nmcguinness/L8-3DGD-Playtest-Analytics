Lab 02 — Comparing Builds

Stage 2: cleaning, t-tests, and whether a difference matters

NoteGet the files
  • Data: tidebreak_sessions_raw.csv, tidebreak_sessions.csv, tidebreak_paired.csv, tidebreak_frame_times.csv, tidebreak_survey.csv. Save them in the data/ folder of your project.

Overview

Build B added lighting and signage to guide players through Tidebreak. This lab asks the question the team built it to answer:

Does improved environmental guidance help players navigate the level?

Part A takes the raw export the team received and gets it into a shape that can answer that question, keeping a written record of every decision. Part B puts a size, an interval and a p-value on the differences, and asks the question no function answers: does this difference matter? By the end you will have answered the Stage 2 question, did the change work?, and written the recommendation.

Function What it does
distinct() Drop rows that repeat an earlier row exactly
is.na() Find missing values
trimws(), tolower() Make text labels consistent
\|> Pass the result on its left into the function on its right
filter(), select(), mutate(), arrange() Keep rows, keep columns, change a column, sort
count(), group_by(), summarise() Counts, and one row of summaries per group
t.test() Independent-samples and paired t-tests, with confidence intervals

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lab you can:

  • find duplicates, gaps and inconsistent labels in a raw export, and fix them in one readable pipeline;
  • write a cleaning log that records every row dropped, why, and what it did to n;
  • explain a p-value by reading a shuffle of the build labels;
  • run an independent-samples and a paired t-test, and pull the estimate, interval and p-value out of the result;
  • tell “no meaningful difference” apart from “cannot tell” using a confidence interval;
  • decide whether a statistically significant difference justifies a design change, and write the recommendation.

Ground rules

  • Clean before you describe. Every change you make to the data goes in your cleaning log, including changes that remove no rows.
  • Look at the plot before running the test. Every Part B exercise assumes you have looked at the data first.
  • Type the code. Open the solution after your attempt, even when your attempt worked.
  • Every exercise ends with a short written answer. Write it. The Challenge is judged on the writing, not the code.
  • When you report a difference, give its size and its interval, not only the p-value.

One defensible answer: keep every row, and handle the gaps per analysis.

Step Rows after Why
Raw export 124 As delivered
Remove exact duplicates 120 4 sessions exported twice; no key duplicates with different values
Standardise cohort 120 18 labels had stray spaces or inconsistent capitals
Keep blank session_minutes 120 All 8 are Build A frustration quits. Summaries of session length exclude them and say so
Keep blank deaths 120 3 rows, no pattern. Excluded from deaths summaries only

What makes it defensible. The blank session lengths are not random. Recorded Build A frustration quits have a median of 11.2 minutes, so the missing ones are almost certainly short, and Build A’s median of 26.1 minutes is an overestimate. Keeping the rows keeps their quit_reason, which is complete and carries the finding. The log says what every summary of session length is missing, so a reader can judge it.

What would make it indefensible. Dropping every incomplete row by reflex, which silently takes n from 120 to 109 and removes the frustration quits the comparison is about. Or filling the blanks with 0 or with Build A’s average, which invents session lengths and hides the logging fault.

Write 3 sentences: the blank session lengths are also a bug in Build A’s telemetry. Who should hear about it, and what goes in the bug report?

Exercise 08 — Could it be chance? Read the shuffle [Do]

If the signposting made no difference, the build label on each session would be arbitrary. This code shuffles the labels 10,000 times, recomputes the gap in mean wrong turns each time, and counts how many shuffled gaps are at least as large as the real one. You do not need to be able to write it. Run it, and compare the result with Exercise 07’s p-value.

gap <- function(y, g) mean(y[g == "A"]) - mean(y[g == "B"])

observed <- gap(sessions$wrong_turns, sessions$build)
set.seed(1)
shuffled <- replicate(10000, gap(sessions$wrong_turns, sample(sessions$build)))

observed
[1] 3.4
max(abs(shuffled))
[1] 2.166667
mean(abs(shuffled) >= abs(observed))   # the shuffle's p-value
[1] 0
hist(shuffled, breaks = 40, col = "grey85", border = "white",
     xlim = range(c(shuffled, observed)),
     main = "Gaps from shuffled build labels",
     xlab = "Difference in mean wrong turns (A - B)")
abline(v = observed, col = "firebrick", lwd = 3)
NoteShow solution

The real gap is 3.4 wrong turns; the largest of ten thousand shuffled gaps is 2.17. No shuffle came close, so the shuffle’s p-value is below 1 in 10,000, which agrees with the t-test’s tiny p-value. The shuffle shows what a p-value is: the share of a world without a difference that looks at least as extreme as your data.

Write 2 sentences: what does one shuffle represent, and why does the proportion of extreme shuffles answer “could this be chance?”

Exercise 10 — No difference, or cannot tell? [Apply]

Run the t-test on enjoyment by build and on items_collected by build. Neither is significant. For which one can you say there is no meaningful difference, and for which can you only say we cannot tell?

Read each interval against the size of the variable. Enjoyment’s interval runs from -0.35 to 0.35 on a seven-point scale: whatever the true difference is, it is too small to matter. That is no meaningful difference. The items interval runs from -1.01 to 0.34 items, when a typical player collects about 4. Build B’s players could be collecting up to 1.0 more items each, or up to 0.3 fewer. That is cannot tell.

“Cannot tell” means this playtest was too small to see a difference of that size. A bigger playtest, or a paired one, would narrow the interval enough to decide (Testing in Depth).

Write 2 sentences: why is “not significant” a weaker statement than “the confidence interval is narrow and sits around zero”?

Exercise 11 — “Players play longer in Build B” [Decide]

t.test(session_minutes ~ build) gives p = 0.037, and Build B’s sessions are about 3 minutes longer on average. The producer wants to put “players play 3 minutes longer in Build B” in the pitch. Do you let them? Test before you decide: compare session length between the builds for players who did not quit in frustration.

NoteShow solution 
not_frustrated <- sessions |> filter(quit_reason != "Frustrated")
t.test(session_minutes ~ build, data = not_frustrated)

One defensible answer: no, because the claim describes the wrong thing. Among players who did not quit in frustration, Build A averaged 26.2 minutes and Build B 25.5 (p = 0.53). Frustration quits end at a median of about 11.7 minutes in both builds. Build B does not make anyone play longer. It makes fewer people quit early, and that pulls its average up. The honest pitch line is “fewer players quit in frustration”, which is also the stronger claim.

What makes it defensible. It checks what is driving the difference before describing it, and it replaces the claim with one the data supports.

What would make it indefensible. Approving the line because the p-value is below 0.05. The difference is real; the explanation attached to it is not.

Write 3 sentences: a significant difference in a mean can come from every player shifting a little or from a few players shifting a lot. How do you tell which, and why does it change the design conclusion?

Exercise 12 — Which number goes in the report? [Decide]

You have three estimates of how much the signposting reduced wrong turns: the session-level test from Exercise 07, the paired study from Exercise 09, and a player-level test that averages each player’s 4 sessions first. Run the player-level test, then decide which result leads the report.

NoteShow solution 
per_player <- sessions |>
  group_by(player_id, build) |>
  summarise(wrong_turns = mean(wrong_turns), .groups = "drop")
player_test <- t.test(wrong_turns ~ build, data = per_player)

attr(,"conf.level")
[1] 0.95
result$conf.int
[1] 2.480141 4.319859

paired_test$conf.int
[1] 1.838014 3.695319
attr(,"conf.level")

One defensible answer: lead with the player-level result, and cite the paired study as support. The session-level test treats 120 sessions as independent when they come from 30 players, so its interval is narrower than the data justifies. The player-level interval, 2.4 to 4.4 wrong turns, is honest about that. The paired study, a different design with different players, points the same way, which is worth more than any single p-value.

What would make it indefensible. Picking whichever result has the smallest p-value or the largest effect. That is choosing the answer, not the analysis.

Write 2 sentences: why is agreement between two differently designed studies stronger evidence than a very small p-value from one?

The headline, in the six steps.

  1. What happened? Build B’s players took fewer wrong turns, fewer quit in frustration, and more finished.
  2. How large? 3.4 fewer wrong turns per session; 20 frustration quits against 5.
  3. How uncertain? The wrong-turns interval runs from 2.5 to 4.3 fewer.
  4. Could it be chance? Not plausibly: p < 0.001, and the same for frustration and completion.
  5. Does it matter? Yes. Even the smallest plausible reduction is noticeable, and none of it cost enjoyment or score. But the frame-time plot shows a hitch in Build B’s Corridor that the average frame rate cannot see.
  6. What should the team do? Keep the signposting; fix the Corridor hitch before Build B goes forward.

What would make it indefensible. Answering from wrong turns alone, because that is the measure the build was designed to move. Or quoting average FPS as proof that performance was unchanged. Both answers are supported by a real number, and both miss what a player would actually experience.

Write 4 sentences, using What → Pattern → Meaning → Action:

  • What did you measure?
  • Pattern: what does the evidence show, across more than one measure? Give the size and the interval for the main one.
  • Meaning: what does that mean for the player?
  • Action: what exactly do you recommend to the producer?
