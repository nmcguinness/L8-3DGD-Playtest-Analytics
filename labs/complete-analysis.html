Lab 05 — From Evidence to Report

Stage 4 workshop: one complete finding, from Tidebreak and then from your own game

NoteGet the files
  • Data: tidebreak_sessions.csv, tidebreak_survey.csv. Save them in the data/ folder of your project.

Overview

This is the last lab, and it is a workshop. It brings the whole course together into the thing your assessment needs:

One complete finding: research question → relevant data → descriptive, visual and statistical evidence → interpretation → evidence-backed recommendation, written under the report headings.

Part A works through one finding on Tidebreak, quickly: does environmental guidance improve navigation without reducing enjoyment? It adds the two things Stage 4 is about, coding comments and triangulating evidence, and ends with a report.qmd that renders. Part B is your project. Bring your team’s data and your draft report.

Function What it does
pivot_longer() Reshape wide survey columns into long rows
left_join() Add columns from another table, matched on a key
case_when(), grepl() Give each comment a code
`r ` inline code Put a computed number into a sentence
quarto render Turn report.qmd into HTML

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lab you can:

  • summarise every survey item by build, and decide what a result lets you claim;
  • code free-text comments with a short codebook, and count the codes;
  • triangulate telemetry, ratings and comments, and report a disagreement honestly;
  • build a report in Quarto under the assessment headings, with every number computed, and render it;
  • write a complete finding from your own game, from question to recommendation.

Ground rules

  • Part B is the point. Keep Part A moving; the solutions are there to be read if time is short.
  • No number goes into the report by hand. If it is a number, it is inline R.
  • Results reports; Discussion judges. Keep them apart.
  • Every finding ends with an Action someone could start on tomorrow (rule 10).
  • If library(tidyr) fails, run install.packages("tidyr") once in the Console.

Part A — One finding, on Tidebreak

Notes for this part: Interpreting and Reporting Evidence and Combining Evidence.

Exercise 01 — Every survey item, both builds [Apply]

Reshape survey long, join each session’s build onto it, and find the proportion of players agreeing (4 or 5) with each item in each build. Which item moved most between the builds?

NoteShow solution 
long <- survey |>
  pivot_longer(-session_id, names_to = "item", values_to = "rating")

agree_by_build <- long |>
  left_join(select(sessions, session_id, build), by = "session_id") |>
  group_by(item, build) |>
  summarise(agree = mean(rating >= 4), .groups = "drop") |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = build, values_from = agree) |>
  mutate(change = B - A) |>
  arrange(desc(abs(change)))

agree_by_build
# A tibble: 8 × 4
  item                     A     B  change
  <chr>                <dbl> <dbl>   <dbl>
1 objective_clear     0.0833 0.383  0.3   
2 match_length        0.7    0.817  0.117 
3 death_understood    0.267  0.167 -0.1   
4 controls_responsive 0.717  0.8    0.0833
5 level_navigable     0.35   0.433  0.0833
6 would_play_again    0.5    0.517  0.0167
7 difficulty_fair     0.317  0.3   -0.0167
8 visual_style        0.933  0.933  0

pivot_longer() makes item a column so one group_by() covers every item. pivot_wider() puts the two builds side by side so the change is one subtraction (Reshaping and Joining).

Write 2 sentences: objective_clear moved most. Which item would you have expected to move most, given what Build B changed, and did it?

NoteShow answer

level_navigable, since Build B added guidance to help players navigate. It moved from 35% agreeing to 43%, against objective_clear’s 8% to 38%. Build B cut wrong turns, but what players noticed was knowing where they were meant to go.

Exercise 02 — “Without reducing enjoyment” [Decide]

The research question has two halves. Test the second: did Build B reduce enjoyment? Use enjoyment from sessions (1–7) and would_play_again from Exercise 01, and decide what the report may claim.

NoteShow solution 
enjoy <- t.test(enjoyment ~ build, data = sessions)
enjoy$estimate
mean in group A mean in group B 
       3.933333        3.933333 
enjoy$conf.int
[1] -0.3483742  0.3483742
attr(,"conf.level")
[1] 0.95
agree_by_build |> filter(item == "would_play_again")
# A tibble: 1 × 4
  item                 A     B change
  <chr>            <dbl> <dbl>  <dbl>
1 would_play_again   0.5 0.517 0.0167

One defensible answer: the report may claim no meaningful reduction. Mean enjoyment was 3.93 in Build A and 3.93 in Build B. The 95% confidence interval for the difference runs from -0.35 to 0.35 on a seven-point scale, so any true difference is small. Agreement with would_play_again was 50% and 52%. Two different measures agree.

What makes it defensible. It rests on an interval, not on “not significant”, and on two measures rather than one. It claims no meaningful reduction, which the evidence supports, rather than no effect, which no test can show.

What would make it indefensible. “The p-value is above 0.05, so Build B has no effect on enjoyment.” Or “Build B is just as enjoyable” from one measure with no interval.

Write 2 sentences: both measures were taken straight after one session. What would you need to know before claiming Build B is as enjoyable over a week of play?

Exercise 04 — Do the sources agree? [Decide]

The telemetry says Build B players took far fewer wrong turns. Compare that with the comment codes from Exercise 03, by build and then by whether the player enjoyed the session (enjoyment >= 5). Decide what the report should say about navigation, and which evidence it should trust for what.

NoteShow solution 
sessions |>
  group_by(build) |>
  summarise(mean_wrong_turns = mean(wrong_turns))
# A tibble: 2 × 2
  build mean_wrong_turns
  <chr>            <dbl>
1 A                 8.92
2 B                 5.52
coded |> count(code, build)
# A tibble: 8 × 3
  code            build     n
  <chr>           <chr> <int>
1 Difficulty      A        19
2 Difficulty      B        16
3 Found the way   A        12
4 Found the way   B         9
5 General         A         6
6 General         B         4
7 Lost or unclear A        23
8 Lost or unclear B        31
coded |> count(code, enjoyed = enjoyment >= 5)
# A tibble: 4 × 3
  code            enjoyed     n
  <chr>           <lgl>   <int>
1 Difficulty      FALSE      35
2 Found the way   TRUE       21
3 General         TRUE       10
4 Lost or unclear FALSE      54

The sources disagree. Build B players took fewer wrong turns, yet 31 Build B comments were coded lost or unclear, against 23 in Build A. Split by enjoyment instead of build, the codes separate cleanly: every lost or unclear comment came from a player who rated enjoyment below 5, and every found the way comment from one who rated it 5 or above.

One defensible answer. Trust the telemetry for what happened: Build B reduced wrong turns. Treat the comments as evidence about how the session felt: they track enjoyment, not build. Report both, in Results, and explain the gap in the Discussion. Recommend structured observation at the junctions in the next playtest, because nothing in this data shows whether players notice when they are lost.

What would make it indefensible. Leaving the comments out because they spoil the story, or reporting them as evidence that Build B made navigation worse.

Write 4 sentences, using What → Pattern → Meaning → Action, for the disagreement.

Exercise 06 — Discussion, Conclusion and Executive Summary [Decide]

Write the last three pieces of the Tidebreak report. Every number is inline R.

  • Discussion: whether the reduction in wrong turns matters to the player; the disagreement from Exercise 04; and at least three limitations specific to this playtest.
  • Conclusion: one finding in What → Pattern → Meaning → Action form that answers the research question.
  • Executive Summary: the question, the finding and the action, in three sentences. Write it last.
NoteShow solution 
wt <- t.test(wrong_turns ~ build, data = sessions)
wt$estimate
mean in group A mean in group B 
       8.916667        5.516667 
wt$conf.int
[1] 2.480141 4.319859
attr(,"conf.level")
[1] 0.95
sessions |> count(cohort, build)
# A tibble: 6 × 3
  cohort  build     n
  <chr>   <chr> <int>
1 Novice  A        18
2 Novice  B        15
3 Regular A        26
4 Regular B        27
5 Veteran A        16
6 Veteran B        18

One defensible Discussion.

A reduction of 3.4 wrong turns per session is large enough for players to notice, and enjoyment did not fall. The comments disagree with the telemetry, but they track enjoyment rather than build, so we treat them as evidence about how sessions felt, not about navigation.

Limitations:

  • Each of the 30 players contributed 4 sessions, so the 120 sessions are not independent; the result holds when each player counts once.
  • Every measure was taken after one session. Nothing here shows whether the difference lasts.
  • Cohorts were not perfectly balanced between the builds; the cohort table is in the appendices so the reader can judge.
  • No observation was recorded, so we cannot say whether players noticed when they were lost.

One defensible Conclusion.

What. Wrong turns per session in 120 sessions, and enjoyment on a seven-point scale, compared between Build A and Build B.

Pattern. Build B players averaged 5.5 wrong turns against Build A’s 8.9, a reduction of between 2.5 and 4.3 per session at 95% confidence. Enjoyment was unchanged, with a mean of 3.93 in both builds.

Meaning. The guidance works, and costs nothing players noticed.

Action. Keep Build B’s guidance and carry it into the next build. Retest with an on-screen objective added, since objective clarity was the survey item that moved most, and add an observation sheet at the first two junctions.

One defensible Executive Summary.

Build B’s environmental guidance cut wrong turns by about 3.4 per session without reducing enjoyment. Players’ comments did not reflect the improvement, because they track how a session felt rather than where the player went. Keep the guidance, add an on-screen objective, and observe the junctions in the next playtest.

What makes it defensible. The limitations are specific to this data, and say what was checked. The Conclusion’s Action follows from its Meaning, and its Pattern gives a size and an interval. The Executive Summary stands on its own.

What would make it indefensible. “The sample was small” as the only limitation; a Conclusion whose Action (“improve the level”) no one could start on; or an Executive Summary that describes the method instead of the finding.

Write 3 sentences: which limitation would most change your Action if it turned out to matter, and how would you test it?

Part B — One complete finding from your own game [Decide]

This is the part that matters. Allow at least half the workshop for it.

Work in your team’s report.qmd. Pick one design question your playtest can answer, and take it all the way through:

  1. Research question (Introduction). One sentence a designer would recognise.
  2. Relevant data (Methodology). Which measures, from which sources, on how many players and sessions. Point to your cleaning log.
  3. Evidence (Results). Descriptive: a summary per group, with n. Visual: one figure with a caption that states the takeaway (Lab 03). Statistical: an interval, and a test if the question is a comparison (Lab 02) or a correlation checked within groups if it is a relationship (Lab 04). Qualitative: coded comments or observation, if you collected them.
  4. Interpretation (Discussion). Does it matter to the player? Do the sources agree? What could make it wrong?
  5. Recommendation (Conclusion). What → Pattern → Meaning → Action. An Action someone on your team could start on tomorrow.

Render the report after each step, not just at the end.

NoteShow answer

There is no single right finding: it depends on your game. Check your finding against the Nine-Part Evidence Workflow before you call it done.

Exercises 01 to 06 are a worked version of the whole process on Tidebreak.

What to leave with

  • One complete finding from your own game, from question to recommendation.
  • A list of what is still missing for the final report, from the checklist above.
